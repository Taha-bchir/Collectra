\chapter*{Abstract}
\phantomsection
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

This report presents the final-year internship project carried out at
\textbf{PeakSoft GmbH} (Wuppertal, Germany) under the title \textbf{Collectra} --
a web-based platform for debt collection management and customer reminder campaigns.

Debt collection teams commonly rely on fragmented tools: spreadsheets for data
storage, email threads for communication history, and general-purpose Customer Relationship Management systems
that provide no lifecycle tracking for outstanding debts.
Collectra addresses these gaps by bringing debt records, campaigns, team actions,
and customer communication into a single, workspace-based Software as a Service environment.

The platform delivers secure JSON Web Token-based authentication with Hypertext Transfer Protocol-only
session cookies,
strict workspace-level data isolation, role-based access control for managers and
agents, a complete debt lifecycle from import through payment, time-limited secure
personal customer links, Stripe-backed payment confirmation with idempotent
verification, automated invoice generation and delivery via Brevo, campaign-level
email tracking analytics, and an operational dashboard for managers.

Development followed the Scrum agile methodology across four two-week sprints, with
task tracking managed in Linear.
The application is deployed on \textbf{Vercel} under the domain
\texttt{collectra.xyz}, using a GitHub-based Continuous Integration and Continuous Deployment pipeline where changes are
merged from the \texttt{develop} branch into \texttt{production} and published
automatically.
The technical stack is built on Next.js, React, TypeScript, Hono, PostgreSQL
(Supabase), and Prisma Object-Relational Mapper, organized in a Turborepo monorepo.

\chapter*{General Introduction}
\phantomsection
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{General Introduction}
% =============================================================================

Debt collection is a critical business process for financial institutions, telecom
operators, and service companies that manage large portfolios of unpaid invoices.
Despite its importance, many collection teams still rely on fragmented tools:
spreadsheets for data storage, email threads for communication history, and separate
Customer Relationship Management systems that were never designed for debt lifecycle tracking.
The consequences are predictable: poor traceability, weak team collaboration, and no
single operational view of campaign progress.

The \textbf{Collectra} project was developed during this internship at PeakSoft GmbH
specifically to address these gaps.
Collectra is a Software as a Service web platform that brings debt data, campaigns, team activities,
and customer communication into one secure, workspace-based environment.
It replaces scattered tools with a single, role-aware application that supports the
full debt lifecycle, from initial import to payment confirmation and invoice delivery.

From a technical perspective, Collectra is built on a modern TypeScript-first monorepo.
The frontend uses Next.js and React, the backend uses Hono with a validated OpenAPI
contract and Zod schemas, and the database is managed with Prisma Object-Relational Mapper on PostgreSQL
via Supabase.
Development was organized with the Scrum agile framework and tracked in Linear over
four two-week sprints.
The platform is deployed on Vercel at \texttt{collectra.xyz} through an automated
GitHub-based Continuous Integration and Continuous Deployment pipeline.

The internship followed professional practices: version control with Git, code review through
pull requests, task tracking in Linear, and automated deployments to a production domain.
Each increment was demonstrated to PeakSoft stakeholders before being merged, which reduced
the risk of building features that did not match real collection workflows.

\section{Project Context}

\subsection{Main Issue}

Debt collection teams face a recurring set of operational challenges.
Follow-up work is often fragmented across multiple tools: debt data is stored in
spreadsheets, contact history is tracked in email threads, and reminder statuses are
maintained manually.
This makes it difficult to audit past actions, share information across team members,
or monitor campaign progress in real time.

The specific problems identified at the start of the project were:
\begin{itemize}
  \item scattered debt data without a single source of truth;
  \item weak traceability of reminders, promises, and payments;
  \item limited collaboration between agents and managers;
  \item no unified view of campaign progress or debt lifecycle status;
  \item no secure way for customers to consult their own debt information.
\end{itemize}

The central question is therefore: \emph{how can we build a secure, scalable platform
that centralizes debt collection work while keeping data strictly isolated per workspace?}

\subsection{Stakeholder Expectations}

During requirements workshops at PeakSoft, three expectations guided the product direction.
\textbf{Managers} needed a reliable overview of campaign progress, team activity, and payment
outcomes without exporting spreadsheets.
\textbf{Agents} needed fast daily operations: importing debts, updating statuses, generating
customer links, and recording promises with minimal friction.
\textbf{Customers} needed a simple, trustworthy page to understand their debt and complete
payment without creating an account.
These expectations translated directly into the sprint backlog and into non-functional
requirements (security, traceability, and performance under bulk import).

\subsection{Analysis of Existing Solutions}

A study of available tools revealed the following limitations:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Spreadsheets (Excel, Google Sheets):} flexible but difficult to audit,
        with no role governance, no lifecycle tracking, and no secure customer access;
  \item \textbf{Generic Customer Relationship Management tools (Salesforce, HubSpot):} designed for sales pipelines,
        not debt status lifecycles; often too complex or costly for small collection teams;
  \item \textbf{Manual link sharing :} no expiration control,
        no authentication, and no audit trail.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Overview of the Proposed Solution}

The proposed solution is \textbf{Collectra}, a workspace-based Software as a Service platform that
covers all five critical capabilities that existing tools only address partially,
as shown in Table~\ref{tab:comparison}.

Collectra offers:
\begin{itemize}
  \item secure authentication and role-based access control;
  \item workspace-level data isolation for multi-team environments;
  \item campaign creation and bulk Comma-Separated Values debt import;
  \item a full debt lifecycle (imported $\to$ notified $\to$ promised $\to$ paid / overdue);
  \item JSON Web Token-secured personal customer links with configurable expiration;
  \item an operational dashboard for managers.
\end{itemize}

\section{Collectra Platform Requirements}

\subsection{Identification of the Actors}

The Collectra platform involves four main actors:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Owner / Manager:} responsible for workspace setup, team invitations,
        campaign supervision, and dashboard monitoring;
  \item \textbf{Agent:} performs daily debt follow-up, records actions and promises,
      and generates customer personal links; agents may also create new workspaces and
      will be assigned the Owner role for any workspace they create by default;
  \item \textbf{Customer:} accesses personal debt information through a secure,
        time-limited link without needing an account;
  \item \textbf{System services:} the Supabase authentication provider and the
        email notification service (Brevo).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Functional Requirements}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Secure user authentication and session management;
  \item Workspace creation and strict workspace-based data isolation;
  \item Team invitation and role-based permission assignment;
  \item Campaign creation, configuration, and lifecycle management;
  \item Bulk Comma-Separated Values import for debt records;
  \item Debt status tracking across a defined lifecycle;
  \item Generation and management of JSON Web Token-secured customer personal links (48-hour expiration by default);
  \item Customer debt consultation via personal link (no account required);
  \item Stripe payment confirmation with idempotent verification;
  \item Invoice generation and delivery after confirmed payment;
  \item Dashboard indicators for campaign and debt monitoring;
  \item Action history log for full traceability;
  \item Campaign-level email tracking analytics.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Non-Functional Requirements}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Security:} JSON Web Tokens signed with Hash-based Message Authentication Code SHA-256, Hypertext Transfer Protocol-only cookies,
        and route-level middleware checks for every protected endpoint;
  \item \textbf{Maintainability:} modular monorepo architecture with shared packages
        and a single Prisma schema as the source of truth;
  \item \textbf{Performance:} efficient handling of bulk Comma-Separated Values imports with Prisma
        transaction batching to avoid database timeouts;
  \item \textbf{Reliability:} validated Application Programming Interface contracts via Zod, database-level constraints,
        and idempotent payment confirmation logic;
  \item \textbf{Usability:} clean dashboard workflow with a minimal learning curve,
        validated through iterative stakeholder feedback during each sprint review.
\end{itemize}

\section{Quality Assurance and Testing Strategy}
\label{sec:qa}

Quality assurance was integrated into every sprint rather than postponed to the end
of the internship.
The approach combined \textbf{functional tests} (documented in each sprint chapter),
\textbf{contract validation} with Zod on the backend, and \textbf{manual exploratory
testing} on Vercel preview deployments before merging to the production branch.

\subsection{Testing Levels}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Unit-level checks:} validation helpers, status-transition guards, and
        payment verification utilities were exercised with representative inputs and edge cases;
  \item \textbf{Integration tests:} Application Programming Interface endpoints were verified
        with Postman collections and Swagger UI, focusing on authentication cookies, workspace
        isolation, and import summaries;
  \item \textbf{End-to-end flows:} login, workspace invitation, Comma-Separated Values import,
        customer personal link access, Stripe checkout, and invoice retrieval were replayed on
        preview environments after each sprint review;
  \item \textbf{Regression discipline:} critical paths from earlier sprints were re-tested when
        shared middleware or database schema changed.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Test Data and Acceptance Criteria}

Each sprint defined explicit acceptance criteria in Linear (expected status codes, visible
user interface states, and database side effects).
For imports, tests included files with delimiter variations, duplicate emails, invalid amounts,
and large batches to confirm chunked inserts remained stable.
For payments, tests covered duplicate verification calls to confirm idempotent confirmation.

\subsection{Defect Management}

Issues discovered during reviews were logged in Linear with severity, reproduction steps,
and the affected module (\texttt{apps/web}, \texttt{apps/api}, or shared packages).
Blocking defects were resolved before merging \texttt{develop} into \texttt{production};
non-blocking improvements were deferred to the backlog with clear priority labels.

\section{Security Architecture}
\label{sec:security}

Security was treated as a cross-cutting concern from Sprint~1 onward.
Collectra combines authentication, authorization, and tenant isolation at multiple layers.

\subsection{Authentication and Session Model}

Users authenticate through Supabase Auth.
The backend issues a signed JSON Web Token stored in a \textbf{Hypertext Transfer Protocol-only}
session cookie so client-side scripts cannot read it.
Protected routes verify the token on every request before executing business logic.

\subsection{Workspace Isolation and Role-Based Access Control}

All operational data is scoped to a \texttt{workspaceId}.
Middleware rejects cross-tenant access attempts with \texttt{403 Forbidden} even when a user
is authenticated.
Managers and agents receive different permissions: sensitive actions (invitations, campaign
deletion, dashboard access) require the manager role.

\subsection{Customer-Facing Security}

Personal links rely on signed JSON Web Tokens with expiration.
Public endpoints never expose internal identifiers without verification.
Payment confirmation uses Stripe session identifiers, database-level locks, and idempotent
verification to prevent duplicate charges.

\subsection{Secrets and Deployment Hygiene}

Database credentials, JSON Web Token secrets, Stripe keys, and Brevo credentials are injected
as environment variables in Vercel.
No secret is committed to Git.
Preview deployments use separate configuration so test keys cannot affect production data.

\section{General Architecture of the Application}

Collectra is structured as a \textbf{monorepo} managed with pnpm and Turborepo.
It contains four main packages:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{apps/web} -- the Next.js frontend application;
  \item \texttt{apps/api} -- the Hono backend Representational State Transfer Application Programming Interface;
  \item \texttt{packages/db} -- the shared Prisma client and database schema;
  \item \texttt{packages/types} -- shared TypeScript types and Zod validation schemas.
\end{itemize}

The logical architecture separates four main domains: authentication and session
management, workspace and team management, campaign and debt management, and customer
access.
Each domain maps to a dedicated set of Application Programming Interface routes and frontend pages.
All inter-domain communication passes through typed Zod-validated contracts, ensuring
that the Application Programming Interface surface is consistent and that both frontend and
backend always share the same type definitions.

\subsection{Monorepo Structure and Code Organization}

The monorepo is orchestrated with Turborepo task pipelines (\texttt{build}, \texttt{lint},
\texttt{dev}) so unchanged packages are skipped during continuous integration.
Shared types in \texttt{packages/types} prevent drift between the Next.js client and the Hono
server: when a request schema changes, TypeScript compilation fails until both sides are updated.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Frontend (\texttt{apps/web}):} route groups for authentication, workspace
        dashboards, campaign screens, and customer-facing public pages;
  \item \textbf{Backend (\texttt{apps/api}):} route modules grouped by domain (auth, workspaces,
        campaigns, debts, public customer access, payments);
  \item \textbf{Database (\texttt{packages/db}):} Prisma schema, migrations, and generated client;
  \item \textbf{Shared contracts (\texttt{packages/types}):} Zod schemas reused for validation and
        OpenAPI generation.
\end{itemize}

This layout reduced duplication and made refactors safer: a single schema change propagates to
the Application Programming Interface contract, the database layer, and the user interface forms
in one coordinated change set.

\subsection{Data Persistence and Domain Entities}

PostgreSQL stores all business entities with strict foreign keys between workspaces, campaigns,
debts, promises, and history tables.
Prisma enforces referential integrity and provides typed queries for dashboard aggregations and
bulk imports.
Customer-facing actions are persisted in history tables so managers can audit who changed a
status, when a promise was recorded, and when a payment was confirmed.

\section{Deployment and Continuous Integration / Continuous Deployment Pipeline}
\label{sec:deployment}

\subsection{Hosting Infrastructure}

Collectra is deployed on \textbf{Vercel}, a cloud platform optimized for Next.js
applications, under the custom domain \texttt{collectra.xyz}.
The Supabase-managed PostgreSQL database runs on Supabase's cloud infrastructure,
and all environment variables (database connection strings, JSON Web Token secrets, Stripe keys,
Brevo application programming interface key) are configured as Vercel project environment variables and are never
committed to the repository.

\subsection{Git Branching Strategy}

The project uses a two-branch Git workflow on GitHub:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\texttt{develop}:} the active development branch where all feature
        branches are merged after code review. This branch reflects the latest
        integrated work-in-progress state.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{production}:} the stable branch that drives live deployments.
        A pull request from \texttt{develop} into \texttt{production} is opened at
        the end of each sprint after all sprint tests pass.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Automated Deployment Pipeline}

Vercel is connected directly to the GitHub repository.
Every merge into the \texttt{production} branch triggers an automatic Vercel build
and deployment, as described in the following steps:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Developer pushes feature work to a feature branch and opens a pull request into \texttt{develop}.
  \item After review, the feature branch is merged into \texttt{develop}.
  \item At sprint end, \texttt{develop} is merged into \texttt{production} via a pull request.
  \item Vercel detects the push to \texttt{production}, runs the Turborepo build pipeline,
        and publishes the updated application to \texttt{collectra.xyz} automatically.
  \item Vercel also generates a unique preview Uniform Resource Locator for every pull request, enabling
        stakeholder review before merging to production.
\end{enumerate}

This pipeline ensures that the live environment always reflects a tested, reviewed
state, and that every deployment is traceable to a specific Git commit.

\section{End-to-End User Journeys}
\label{sec:user-journeys}

Beyond isolated screens, Collectra supports three repeatable journeys that structure the
internship demonstrations and acceptance tests.

\subsection{Manager Journey}

A manager authenticates, creates or selects a workspace, invites agents, and configures campaigns.
After agents import debts, the manager monitors progress from the dashboard, filters by campaign,
and reviews action history for audit purposes.
When payments occur, the manager verifies invoice availability and email tracking indicators.
This journey validates workspace isolation, role-based access control, and reporting value.

\subsection{Agent Journey}

An agent logs in, opens an assigned campaign, imports or updates debts, and records promises
during customer calls.
The agent generates personal links, shares them through Brevo-powered communication, and updates
statuses as outcomes arrive.
The journey emphasizes speed: most actions are reachable in two or three clicks from the campaign
debt table.

\subsection{Customer Journey}

A customer receives a signed link, opens the public debt page without an account, reviews the
amount and due information, optionally submits a promise date, and may start Stripe Checkout.
After payment, the customer is redirected to verification and can open the hosted invoice.
This journey validates public Application Programming Interface endpoints, token expiration, and
idempotent payment confirmation.

\subsection{Sprint 1 Business Logic}

\subsubsection{Authentication}

Authentication is handled by Supabase Auth using email and password.
On a successful login, the backend sets an Hypertext Transfer Protocol-only session cookie containing the
user's JSON Web Token.
A Next.js middleware layer checks this cookie on every protected route and page,
redirecting unauthenticated requests to the login screen.
This approach prevents token theft via JavaScript and protects all workspace data
behind a single, centrally managed guard.

\subsubsection{Workspace Isolation}

Every Application Programming Interface endpoint that accesses workspace resources validates a \texttt{workspaceId}
guard before processing any data.
The system verifies that the authenticated user is an active member of the requested
workspace, ensuring that data from one workspace is never visible to users of another.

\subsubsection{Invitation Flow}

When a manager invites a user, the system:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item generates a unique, time-limited invitation token;
  \item stores the invitation record with status \textit{Pending} in the database;
  \item sends an email containing a signed acceptance link;
  \item on acceptance, creates the workspace membership with the assigned role
        and marks the invitation as \textit{Accepted}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Sprint 2 Business Logic}

\subsubsection{Debt Lifecycle}

The debt lifecycle follows a strict sequence of status transitions:

\begin{center}
  \texttt{Imported} $\;\to\;$ \texttt{Notified} $\;\to\;$ \texttt{Promised}
  $\;\to\;$ \texttt{Paid}
\end{center}

A debt may also move to \texttt{OVERDUE\_AFTER\_PROMISE} if the recorded due date or a promise date passes without a payment being confirmed.
The backend enforces allowed transitions and prevents invalid jumps; this logic is implemented in the Debts and Overdue services (\texttt{apps/api/src/services/debts.ts}, \texttt{apps/api/src/services/overdue-debts.ts}).

\subsubsection{Comma-Separated Values Import Logic}

The Comma-Separated Values import endpoint implements a tolerant, production-ready importer:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item receives the file as a multipart/form-data upload;
  \item detects the delimiter (comma, semicolon, or tab) and parses quoted values robustly;
  \item resolves header aliases (for example \texttt{fullname}, \texttt{name}, \texttt{clientname} $\to$ full name; \texttt{montant}/\texttt{amount} $\to$ amount) and validates required columns (full name, email, phone, amount);
  \item normalizes fields (email lowercasing, amount parsing) and maps textual status values using a canonical mapping (see \texttt{STATUS\_MAPPING} in \texttt{apps/api/src/services/campaigns.ts});
  \item matches existing clients by case-insensitive email; for unseen emails the importer prepares a batch of pending clients and inserts them using \texttt{createMany} in chunks (safe chunk size used in code: 500);
  \item inserts debts using \texttt{createMany} in chunked batches inside a transaction to avoid timeouts;
  \item returns a detailed import summary (total rows, imported rows, skipped rows with reasons) and a list of per-debt notifications to attempt via Brevo.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{JSON Web Token Customer Links}

Personal links are implemented as signed JSON Web Tokens (Hash-based Message Authentication Code SHA-256). The token
encodes the debt id in the \texttt{sub} claim and is signed using the server secret.
The implementation issues tokens with a default expiry of 30 days (see
\texttt{EXPIRY\_DAYS} in \texttt{apps/api/src/lib/customer-jwt.ts}). Token verification is
stateless (no DB row required) and performed by \texttt{verifyCustomerToken} when the
public link is accessed.

Customer actions performed via the public link (link click, link open, promise
creation, payment initiation) are recorded in \texttt{customerActionHistory} and Brevo
event logs where applicable.

\subsection{Sprint 3 Business Logic}

\subsubsection{Action History}

Every significant operation -- status update, promise recorded, link generated,
member invited -- is written to a \texttt{CustomerActionHistory} table.
Each entry stores the user ID, workspace ID, action type, target entity ID, and a
timestamp.
The history is exposed as a paginated, chronological table on the history screen,
giving managers a complete audit trail of all workspace activity.

\subsubsection{Dashboard Aggregation}

Dashboard queries are computed server-side using Prisma \texttt{groupBy} queries.
Debt records are grouped by campaign and status, returning counts for each combination.
The frontend renders these counts as summary indicator cards and a per-campaign
status breakdown.
Using \texttt{groupBy} at the database level avoids fetching individual records and
eliminates N+1 query patterns that would degrade performance on large campaigns.

\subsubsection{Role-Based Permission Enforcement}

Sensitive actions -- deleting a campaign, modifying workspace settings, changing
member roles -- are protected by a role guard middleware.
The guard reads the requesting user's workspace role from the database and rejects
the request with \texttt{403 Forbidden} if the role is insufficient.
Agents who attempt Manager-only operations receive a clear error response without
any data leak.

\subsection{Sprint 4 Business Logic}

\subsubsection{Payment Confirmation and Idempotency}

The payment pipeline was hardened to prevent duplicate charges and stale frontend states.
After Stripe redirection, the client calls a verification endpoint and polls until the
database confirms the debt as \texttt{PAID}.
Server-side confirmation is idempotent: repeated webhook or verification calls do not
duplicate state transitions.

The backend enforces the following safeguards:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a \texttt{pendingStripeSessionId} lock to block concurrent checkout sessions;
  \item strict eligibility checks before payment start (status and promise-date rules);
  \item idempotent confirmation that updates debt status only once.
\end{itemize}

This prevents duplicate payment initiation and keeps User Interface state aligned with persisted backend data.

\subsubsection{Invoice Generation and Delivery}

Invoice generation occurs only after confirmed payment.
When the debt transitions to \texttt{PAID}, the service creates or reuses a stable
invoice number, exposes a secure public invoice endpoint, and redirects users to the
hosted invoice or printable Portable Document Format representation.

Invoice email is sent as a post-confirmation side effect through Brevo,
ensuring payment state remains the primary source of truth even if email delivery fails.

\subsubsection{Email Tracking and Manager Analytics Synchronization}

Campaign import and communication flows now persist normalized tracking actions
(\texttt{EMAIL\_SENT}, \texttt{LINK\_CLICKED}, and mapped provider events) into action history.
Manager endpoints aggregate these events into operational metrics and refresh them in near real time.

The final dashboard therefore combines:
\begin{itemize}
  \item debt lifecycle indicators;
  \item payment and invoice completion visibility;
  \item communication performance metrics for campaign follow-up decisions.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Cross-Cutting Technical Topics}
\label{chap:crosscutting}
% =============================================================================

\section*{Introduction}
\phantomsection
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Introduction}

While sprint chapters follow delivery order, several mechanisms span the entire platform.
This chapter summarises the email pipeline, payment and invoice flow, error handling philosophy,
and operational practices that support maintainability in production.

\section{Email and Notification Pipeline}

Collectra uses Brevo for transactional email and event tracking.
When agents trigger customer communications, the platform records outbound messages and
synchronises provider events (\texttt{EMAIL\_SENT}, \texttt{LINK\_CLICKED}, opens) into
action history for manager review.
The design keeps email diagnostics separate from core debt state: a failed provider callback
does not corrupt payment status, but the event log helps support teams investigate delivery issues.

\section{Payment, Invoice, and Customer Return Flow}

The customer journey on a personal link follows a controlled sequence:
view debt details, optionally submit a promise-to-pay date, start Stripe Checkout, return to
a verification endpoint, and access a hosted invoice once payment is confirmed.
Server-side locks prevent parallel checkout sessions for the same debt.
Verification is idempotent: repeated calls after success return the same final state without
duplicate charges or duplicate history entries.

\section{Error Handling and User Feedback}

Application Programming Interface errors return structured JSON payloads with stable error codes.
The frontend maps these codes to actionable messages (invalid import row, expired link,
forbidden workspace access).
For long-running imports, the backend returns per-row skip reasons so agents can correct files
instead of failing the entire batch silently.

\section{Performance Considerations}

Large Comma-Separated Values files are processed in chunks inside database transactions.
Dashboard indicators rely on aggregation queries rather than per-debt loops.
Preview deployments on Vercel allowed measuring import duration and dashboard load time before
promoting changes to production.

\section{Documentation and Knowledge Transfer}

OpenAPI documentation is generated from Zod schemas, keeping the contract close to runtime
validation.
Sprint reviews at PeakSoft served as living documentation: each demo explained assumptions,
limitations, and follow-up tasks recorded in Linear for continuity after the internship.

\section*{Conclusion}
\phantomsection
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Conclusion}

These cross-cutting topics complement the sprint-by-sprint narrative.
They highlight how authentication, imports, payments, and communications remain coherent when
implemented as platform-wide rules rather than isolated features.

\chapter*{General Conclusion}

This report presented the full design and implementation of \textbf{Collectra}, a
web platform for debt collection and campaign management, developed during a
final-year internship at PeakSoft GmbH.

\textbf{Summary of achievements.}
The project successfully delivered a complete, production-ready Software as a Service application
across four Scrum sprints.
Sprint 1 established the authentication system and workspace infrastructure.
Sprint 2 introduced campaign management, bulk Comma-Separated Values import, a full debt lifecycle,
and JSON Web Token-secured customer personal links.
Sprint 3 added action history logging, payment promise recording, and the operational
manager dashboard.
Sprint 4 completed the payment-to-invoice pipeline with Stripe verification,
idempotent confirmation, secure invoice access, and synchronized campaign
tracking analytics.
The platform is live on Vercel at \texttt{collectra.xyz}, deployed automatically
via a GitHub-based Continuous Integration and Continuous Deployment pipeline.

\textbf{Non-functional requirements validation.}
Looking back at the requirements defined in Chapter~2, all five non-functional
requirements were met:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Security:} JSON Web Tokens are signed with Hash-based Message Authentication Code SHA-256, stored in
        Hypertext Transfer Protocol-only cookies, and verified by middleware on every protected route.
        Workspace isolation is enforced at every layer, from database queries to
        Application Programming Interface middleware to frontend navigation.
  \item \textbf{Maintainability:} the monorepo structure with a single shared
        Prisma schema and shared Zod types means that any model change propagates
        consistently across frontend and backend without manual synchronization.
  \item \textbf{Performance:} the Comma-Separated Values import uses Prisma transaction batching to
        avoid per-row round trips, and the dashboard uses \texttt{groupBy}
        aggregation to prevent N+1 queries on large campaign data sets.
  \item \textbf{Reliability:} Zod validates every Application Programming Interface input at runtime, and the
        idempotent payment confirmation logic prevents duplicate state transitions
        regardless of how many times the verify endpoint is called.
  \item \textbf{Usability:} iterative sprint reviews with PeakSoft stakeholders
        confirmed that the workflow from campaign creation to payment confirmation
        requires minimal training for new collection agents.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Technical contributions.}
From a technical perspective, the project demonstrates a full-stack TypeScript
monorepo architecture combining Next.js, Hono, Prisma, and Supabase in a
production-grade setup.
The use of Zod for both runtime validation and OpenAPI schema generation, combined
with workspace-level data isolation enforced at every layer, shows how security and
maintainability can be built in from the very start of a project.
The final implementation also demonstrates robust financial-flow design through
duplicate-session prevention, idempotent confirmation logic, and post-transaction
invoice and email side effects.

\textbf{Challenges encountered.}
The main challenges faced during the project were:
\begin{itemize}
  \item designing a flexible but strict debt status transition system that prevents
        invalid lifecycle jumps while remaining easy to extend;
  \item handling large Comma-Separated Values files efficiently without overloading the database
        or causing request timeouts;
  \item securing the Stripe payment return path to avoid duplicate payments while
        keeping customer feedback responsive during webhook delays;
  \item ensuring correct workspace isolation at every layer of the application,
        from database queries to Application Programming Interface middleware to frontend navigation.
\end{itemize}
Each challenge was resolved through careful schema design, server-side validation,
and iterative testing with real data.

\textbf{Personal learning outcomes.}
This internship provided hands-on experience in full-stack TypeScript development,
agile project management with Scrum, and professional software delivery in a real
company context.
It deepened my understanding of authentication flows, role-based access control,
JSON Web Token-based security, real-world relational data modeling, and production deployment
with Vercel and GitHub Continuous Integration and Continuous Deployment pipelines.

\textbf{Perspectives and future work.}
Several improvements could extend the Collectra platform in the future:
\begin{itemize}
  \item integration with SMS or WhatsApp for direct, automated customer notifications;
  \item a scheduled background job for automatic overdue debt detection;
  \item campaign report export in Portable Document Format or Excel format;
  \item a mobile-responsive redesign or dedicated mobile application;
  \item advanced analytics and collection performance reporting for managers.
\end{itemize}

In conclusion, Collectra meets all the objectives set at the beginning of the
internship.
It provides collection teams with a secure, structured, and easy-to-use platform
that replaces fragmented tools with a single, purpose-built solution for debt
management.

\chapter{Domain Glossary}

This glossary defines recurring business and technical terms used in the report.

\begin{description}[style=nextline, leftmargin=3.2cm, labelwidth=2.8cm, itemsep=8pt]
  \item[Workspace] Isolated tenant environment containing campaigns, debts, members, and history.
  \item[Campaign] Collection operation grouping debts that share communication and reporting context.
  \item[Debt] Individual amount owed by a customer, with lifecycle status and payment metadata.
  \item[Personal link] Time-limited, signed Uniform Resource Locator allowing a customer to view or act on a debt without an account.
  \item[Promise to pay] Customer commitment date recorded by an agent or submitted through the public link.
  \item[Action history] Chronological log of operational events (status updates, promises, payments, tracking).
  \item[Idempotent confirmation] Payment verification that yields the same final state when executed multiple times.
  \item[Import summary] Structured result of a Comma-Separated Values upload (imported, skipped, error reasons).
  \item[Manager dashboard] Aggregated indicators (counts by status, campaign filters) for supervision.
  \item[Preview deployment] Temporary Vercel build used to validate a pull request before production merge.
\end{description}
